# Voice Analysis Guide

## How to Extract Voice Patterns

This guide teaches you how to analyze writing samples and extract actionable voice patterns. The goal is not literary analysis . it is practical codification that another AI session can use to replicate the voice.

## The 5 Dimensions of Voice

### 1. Rhythm

**What to look for:**
- Count average words per sentence across all samples
- Note the range (shortest sentence vs. longest)
- Look for deliberate rhythm patterns: short-short-long, or consistent medium
- Check for fragments, one-word sentences, rhetorical questions used as pacing
- Check for em dashes, ellipses, or parentheticals that interrupt flow

**How to codify it:**
- "Sentences average [X] words. Mix short punchy statements (3–8 words) with medium explanatory ones (15–25 words). Rarely go over 30 words."
- "Uses sentence fragments for emphasis. One-word sentences after a setup."
- "Uses em dashes for asides . like this . rather than parentheses."

### 2. Vocabulary

**What to look for:**
- Grade level of vocabulary (simple/accessible vs. elevated/specialized)
- Jargon usage: do they use industry terms or translate them into plain language?
- Recurring words across samples (these are signature words)
- Metaphor patterns: do they use sports metaphors? Building metaphors? War metaphors? Cooking? Nature?
- Contractions: does the writing use "don't" and "can't" or "do not" and "cannot"?

**How to codify it:**
- "Plain language, 8th-grade reading level. No jargon unless explaining it."
- "Uses contractions naturally. Never stiff or formal."
- "Recurring signature words: [list]. These should appear organically."
- "Metaphor style: [type]. Avoid [type they never use]."

### 3. Emotional Register

**What to look for:**
- Baseline warmth: cold/professional, warm/approachable, or hot/intense?
- How they handle personal disclosure (open, guarded, strategic)
- Whether they use humor (dry, self-deprecating, playful, never)
- How they express conviction (bold declarations, qualified statements, rhetorical questions)
- Whether they show vulnerability and how much

**How to codify it:**
- "Default tone: [warm/direct/intense/measured]. Rarely [opposite]."
- "Uses humor sparingly . dry, observational. Never slapstick or forced."
- "Expresses conviction through direct statements, not hedge words. Says 'this is wrong' not 'I think this might not be optimal.'"
- "Personal disclosure level: [high/medium/low]. Shares experiences when they serve the point, not for engagement."

### 4. Structure

**What to look for:**
- Paragraph length: short (1–2 sentences), medium (3–4), or long (5+)?
- How they open content (hook, story, statement, question)
- How they transition between ideas (connective phrases, hard breaks, numbered points)
- How they close (CTA, reflection, punchline, question, callback)
- Whether they use lists, headers, or flowing prose

**How to codify it:**
- "Paragraphs: 1–3 sentences. Lots of white space. Never wall-of-text."
- "Opens with a direct statement or bold claim. Never opens with a question unless it is genuinely surprising."
- "Closes with a one-liner that reframes the whole piece. Not a CTA on every piece."
- "Transitions: uses 'Here is the thing', 'Look', 'But' to start paragraphs. Conversational bridges."

### 5. Distinctive Patterns

**What to look for:**
- Any construction that appears in 2+ samples (this is a signature move)
- A consistent opening or closing pattern
- A specific way they handle objections or counterarguments
- A characteristic use of specificity (names, numbers, places, dates)
- Any pattern that, if removed, would make the writing unrecognizable

**How to codify it:**
- "Signature move: [describe]. Example from samples: '[quote]'."
- "Always includes specific numbers or details when making a claim. Never vague."
- "Handles counterarguments by naming them first, then reframing: 'Most people think X. Here is what is actually happening.'"

## Analysis Quality Standards

**Good analysis:**
- Every observation cites a specific example from the user's writing
- Patterns are identified across multiple samples, not from one outlier
- The analysis is specific enough to distinguish this voice from a similar one
- Rules are actionable . another AI could follow them

**Bad analysis:**
- Vague labels like "professional but approachable" with no supporting evidence
- Observations that could apply to any decent writer
- Rules that are too abstract to act on ("write naturally")
- Missing the distinctive patterns that make this voice unique

## When Samples Are Insufficient

If the user provides fewer than 3 samples or very short samples:
- Ask for more. Be encouraging: "I can work with this, but more samples will make the voice file dramatically better. Can you grab 2–3 more?"
- If they truly cannot provide more, work with what you have but note the limitation in the generated file: "This voice architecture is based on limited samples. As you produce more content, revisit and refine."

## When Samples Are Inconsistent

If the writing style varies significantly across samples:
- Name it constructively: "I am seeing two different registers in your writing. [Sample A] is more [X], while [Sample B] is more [Y]. Which one feels more like the real you . or are both intentional for different contexts?"
- If both are intentional, create TWO voice modes in the architecture file (e.g., "newsletter voice" and "social media voice")
- If one is the real voice and the other was a performative attempt, use the real one

## When the User Cannot Identify Their Voice

Some people genuinely do not know what their voice sounds like. They have never thought about it.

In this case:
- Lean harder on the analysis. YOU tell THEM what their voice is based on the samples.
- Present it with confidence: "Based on your writing, here is who you are on the page."
- Let them react to your description rather than trying to self-describe.
- This is often the most valuable moment . people discovering their own voice for the first time.
